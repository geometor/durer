Demonstrative vs. Mechanice Regular Polygons & the Rusty Compass (Figs. 9–20)¶

Hexagon, Equilateral Triangle & Heptagon (p. 057, Figs. 9–11): First make a six-cornered figure, because the unchanged compass yields it of itself around center a (1..6, euclid:elements IV.15); joining alternate vertices 1, 3, 5 gives the equilateral triangle, and perpendicular diameters b.c. and d.e. with repeated bisections yield the square, octagon, and 16-gon (Figs. 13–14). Now I will make a heptagon from the preceding triangle and its description by a common way used for speed in workshop practice (von behendigkeyt wegen in der arbeyt). I do it thus: I draw a straight line from center a to point .2., which bisects side .1.3. of the triangle in the middle; at that midpoint I set .b., and the length .1.b. steps seven times around the circle (W0, W1, W2, …, W6), from which the 14-gon and 28-gon follow by bisection (Fig. 12).

Pentagon & Decagon after Ptolemy and Euclid (p. 058, Fig. 15): Now it is necessary to construct a pentagon inside a circular ring; do it thus (after Ptolemy): from center a draw a circle, and draw a horizontal diameter through center a; where it cuts the circle on both sides set b and c. Next draw an upright line through center a at right angles, and where it cuts the circle above set d. Divide radius .a.c. at its midpoint with point e, draw a straight line .e.d., take a compass, place one foot at point e and the other at d, and swing an arc down onto the horizontal diameter .b.c.; where it cuts it set .f., and join .f.d. by a straight line. This length .f.d. is the side of a pentagon that steps five times around the circle, while .f.a. is the side of a decagon (demonstrative). Furthermore, from that same midpoint e on .a.c., if you carry an upright line upward to the circumference, you have a seventh part of the circle mechanice.

Pentagon with an Unchanged Compass (p. 059, Fig. 16): To construct a pentagon with an unchanged compass, do it thus: draw two circles through one another so that each circumference passes through the other’s center, and join the two centers a and b with a straight line; this becomes one side of the pentagon. Where the two circular arcs intersect one another, set c above and d below, and draw the straight vertical line .c.d.. Next take the unchanged compass, set one foot in point d, and with the other sweep an arc through both circles and through both centers a and b; where the two circular arcs are cut set e and f, and where the upright .c.d. is cut set g. Then draw a straight line from .e. through .g. all the way out to the right circle and set h; next draw another straight line from .f. through .g. out to the left circle and set i. Join .i.a. and .h.b. by straight lines, giving three sides of the pentagon, and from .i. and .h. let two equal side lengths meet above at k, completing the pentagon (compared below against the Euclidean vertices Hphi, Iphi, Kphi constructed from phi).

15-gon, Nonagon by Three Fish-Bladders, 11-gon, 13-gon & Arc Trisection (pp. 060–061, Figs. 17–20):

  • 15-gon (Fig. 17 — Demonstrative): Subtracting the arc of the inscribed pentagon from that of the equilateral triangle leaves a remainder whose bisection gives the fifteenth part of the circle (euclid:elements IV.16).

  • Nonagon (Fig. 18 — Mechanice): A nine-cornered figure is to be found through a triangle thus: from center .a. draw a large circle, and within it draw three fish-bladders with the unchanged compass; trisect the axis b.a. of the upper lens and draw a perpendicular cross-chord e.f. through the inner division to obtain the side of the nonagon.

  • 11-gon & 13-gon (Fig. 19 — Mechanice): When I wish quickly to draw an eleven-sided figure in a circle, I take a quarter of the circle’s diameter and lengthen it by one-eighth of itself, and step this length around the circle, where it closes closely—so that it is found mechanice, though not demonstrative. Further, when I must swiftly make a thirteen-sided figure, I draw a circle from center .a., draw a half-diameter .a.b., bisect it at point .d., and step the length .c.d. thirteen times around the circle—which is likewise mechanice and not demonstrative.

  • Trisection of a Circular Arc (Fig. 20): Trisecting chord a.b. and refining with the compass cuts any circular arc into three equal parts for workshop practice; whoever desires it more exact, let him seek it demonstrative.
