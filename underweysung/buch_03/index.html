Nuremberg Sundials (49^\circ Pole) & Optical Tower Scaling (Figs. 20–27)

The Nuremberg Analemma & Polyhedral Cube Sundial (pp. 108–113, Figs. 20–27): Wherever you happen to be in any city and wish to construct a horologium (sundial), first find by an instrument, or in tables made for that purpose, how high the pole is elevated above the earth—thus at Nuremberg its elevation is forty-nine degrees (neun vnd vierzig grad). Having divided quadrant b.a.c. into \(90^\circ\) (\(90 = 3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5\), Fig. 21), count upward from .b. to the end of the forty-ninth degree, set .d. there, and draw the straight line .a.d.; this line .a.d. is called in Latin axis mundi, that is, the axis upon which the whole world turns. Next draw another upright line, as you were taught before, from point .d. onto line .a.b. at right angles, and mark that foot .e.; this line is called in Latin muralis and in German Mauerlini, from which upright wall dials are made. Next draw a straight line from point .e. onto line .a.d. at right angles and mark .f.; this line is called equinoctialis, for whatever is added to the pole is taken from the equator, so that forty-nine degrees remain for the pole’s altitude at Nuremberg and the remaining forty-one degrees to the zenith measure the distance from the equator to our horizon. Projecting a 24-hour equinoctial wheel (Fig. 22) onto tangent lines p.q. and r.s. and transferring radii d.e. and a.e. constructs both the vertical South wall dial (an die aufrecht mauer) and the horizontal dial (auf den orizon, Fig. 23), as well as a chamfered polyhedral cube sundial (Figs. 24–25) facing East, West, South, North, the \(49^\circ\) poles, and the \(41^\circ\) equinoctial plane.
