Euclidean Area Transformations & Circle Quadrature (Figs. 28–36)
================================================================

Now it is necessary to know somewhat of how one may make plane figures equal to one another in
content (*vergleichung der ebnen*)—for instance, how to make a triangle and out of it a square that
contains as much area as the triangle:

1. **Squaring Triangles, Rectangles & Regular Polygons (Figs. 28, 32–33 — Demonstrative):**
   Bisect the altitude of a triangle to transform it into an equal oblong ``.a.b.c.d.``. Take two
   sides—one long and one short—from the oblong ``.a.b.c.d.``, join them end to end along a
   horizontal line, and mark their three points ``.e.f.g.``. Set a point ``.h.`` midway on this
   line; take a compass, set one foot in ``.h.``, and with the other sweep a semicircle from ``.e.``
   around to ``.g.``. Then draw a straight upright line from point ``.f.`` up to the circular arc,
   and where it touches set ``.i.``. If you then join four straight lines, each as long as ``.i.f.``,
   at right angles, there arises a true square that contains as much area as the oblong
   (:cite:`euclid:elements` II.14, VI.13), and in the same way any polygon may be squared by dividing
   it into triangles (Fig. 33).
2. **Proportional Scaling & Multiplying Areas (Figs. 29–31, 35):** Extending the diagonal
   (:term:`Ortstrich <ortstrich>`) of an oblong enlarges or diminishes it while keeping the same
   proportion (:cite:`euclid:elements` VI.24, Fig. 29). Using the semicircle mean proportional
   together with the diagonal multiplies any oblong or square twofold, threefold, or sevenfold while
   preserving its shape (Figs. 30–31), and similar figures erected on the sides of a right triangle
   stand in the same equality of area as squares (:cite:`euclid:elements` VI.31, Fig. 35).
3. **Quadrature of the Circle (p. 075, Fig. 34):** It would be necessary to know *quadratura
   circuli*—that is, the equating of a circle and a square so that the one contains as much as the
   other—**yet this has not yet been demonstrated by scholars**. *Mechanice*, however—that is,
   approximately, so that in practical work it misses not at all or only by very little—this
   equality may be made thus: draw a square, divide its diagonal (:term:`ortstrich <ortstrich>`)
   into ten equal parts, and then draw a circle (around :g:`O_q`) whose diameter takes eight of
   those parts, as the diagonal of the square has ten.
