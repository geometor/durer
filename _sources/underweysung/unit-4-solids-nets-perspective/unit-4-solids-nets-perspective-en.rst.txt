Book IV — Regular & Archimedean Nets, Cube Duplication & Perspective
==========================================================================

.. folio:: N1525 p. 142–157

First Printed Planar Fold-Out Nets for Platonic & Archimedean Solids (Figs. 29–43)
==================================================================================

In the opening of Book IV, :person:`Dürer <durer>` classifies three families of solid bodies that
may be fashioned with :term:`compass <zirckel>` and :term:`straightedge <richtscheyt>`: prismatic
columns of uniform cross-section, tapering cones and pyramids, and finally **bodies equal on every
side in their faces, solid corners, and edges**, *"which :person:`Euclid <euclid>` calls
:term:`corpora regularia <corpora-regularia>`, describing five of them because there can be no more
that may be inscribed in a sphere touching it at every vertex"* (:cite:`euclid:elements`,
:cite:`plato:timaeus`).

Whereas :person:`Luca Pacioli <pacioli>` and :person:`Leonardo da Vinci <leonardo>` had depicted
spatial perspective wireframes of polyhedra in *Divina Proportione* (:cite:`pacioli:1509`), Dürer
invents a **transformative medium in the history of mathematics**: for every solid, alongside its
closed ground plan and elevation, he prints the **exact two-dimensional unfolded planar net** (*gantz
aufgethan / offen*) with explicit craft instructions on page 148:

   *"Whoever wishes to make them should draw them larger upon two sheets of paper pasted together
   (zwifach gepabt papier), and with a sharp knife score along all the drawn lines through one ply
   of the paper on one side; and once the whole figure is cut free from the remaining paper, fold
   the body together—it bends willingly along the scored lines."*

1. **The Five Platonic Solids & the Gore Sphere (pp. 142–147, Figs. 29–34)**:

   - **Tetrahedron (Fig. 29)**: $4$ equilateral triangular faces, $4$ three-valent solid corners,
     and $6$ sharp edges.
   - **Octahedron (Fig. 30)**: "shaped like a diamond point" (*wie ein diamant punct*)—$8$
     equilateral triangles, $6$ four-valent corners, and $12$ edges.
   - **Icosahedron (Fig. 31)**: $20$ equilateral triangles, $12$ five-valent corners, and $30$ edges.
   - **Cube (Fig. 32)**: "like a four-cornered die" (*wie ein vierecketer würffel*)—$6$ right
     squares, $8$ three-valent corners, and $12$ edges.
   - **Dodecahedron (Fig. 33)**: $12$ regular pentagonal faces unfolded into **two joined
     six-pentagon rosettes** over $\mathbb{Q}(\phi)$, with $20$ three-valent corners and $30$ edges.
   - **Meridian Gore Sphere (Fig. 34)**: A sphere sliced along its meridians and laid flat "acquires
     the form of a comb" (*ein gestal eins kams*)—the mathematical template of Renaissance printed
     terrestrial and celestial globes.

   :caption: Exact unit-quadrance ($Q = 1$) planar fold-out nets for the Tetrahedron (Fig. 29), Octahedron (Fig. 30), and Cube (Fig. 32), distinguishing interior scored fold hinges (dashed) from outer cut seams (solid).

.. picture:: ../../_static/figures/book4_dodecahedron_net.svg
   :caption: Dürer's double six-pentagon rosette net for the regular Dodecahedron (Book IV, Fig. 33) constructed with 100% exact symbolic coordinates in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5}, \sin 36^\circ)$.

2. **Seven Archimedean Semi-Regular Solids & Composite Nets (pp. 148–157, Figs. 35–43)**:
   Proceeding to solids bounded by **two or three species of regular polygons** whose vertices all
   touch a circumscribed hollow sphere (:person:`Archimedes <archimedes>`, :person:`Pappus <pappus>`
   :cite:`pappus:synagoge`), Dürer publishes planar nets for **seven Archimedean solids**:

   - **Truncated Tetrahedron (Fig. 35)**: $4$ hexagons and $4$ triangles ($12$ vertices, $18$ edges).
   - **Truncated Cube (Fig. 36)**: $6$ octagons and $8$ triangles ($24$ vertices, $36$ edges).
   - **Cuboctahedron (Fig. 37)**: $6$ squares and $8$ triangles ($12$ vertices, $24$ edges).
   - **Truncated Octahedron (Fig. 38)**: $8$ hexagons and $6$ squares ($24$ vertices, $36$ edges).
   - **Rhombicuboctahedron (Fig. 39)**: $18$ squares and $8$ triangles ($24$ vertices, $48$ edges).
   - **Snub Cube (Fig. 40)**: $6$ squares and $32$ equilateral triangles ($24$ vertices, $60$ edges!).
   - **Truncated Cuboctahedron (Fig. 41)**: $6$ octagons, $8$ hexagons, and $12$ squares ($48$
     vertices, $72$ edges!).

.. picture:: ../../_static/figures/book4_cuboctahedron_icosahedron_nets.svg
   :caption: Exact unit-quadrance planar nets over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{3})$ for the Platonic Icosahedron (Fig. 31, left, $20$ triangles) and the Archimedean Cuboctahedron (Fig. 37, right, $6$ squares + $8$ triangles).

The Delian Altar: Doubling the Cube & Cannonball Scaling (Figs. 44–51)
======================================================================

Recounting how the citizens of plague-stricken Athens consulted Apollo's oracle and were commanded
to **double his cubical altar**, Dürer explains how :person:`Plato <plato>` reduced cube duplication
to finding **two mean proportionals** ($g : c = c : d = d : e \implies c^3 = g^2 e$) between two
unequal segments. Declaring that *"because this is a most useful art serving all craftsmen, yet kept
in great secrecy and concealment by scholars, I wish to bring it into the light of day—for as I
believe, it has never until this day been described in the German tongue,"* Dürer presents **three
classical constructions** and applies them directly to casting proportional bells and iron
cannonballs (*püxsen kugeln*) of $1, 2, 3, \dots, 100$ pounds:

1. **Sporus / Pappus Movable-Scale Neusis (pp. 157–163, Figs. 44–49)**: Pivoting a symmetrically
   graduated straightedge at ``d`` until segments ``g.h.`` and ``h.i.`` across vertical axis
   ``a.b.c.`` become equal constructs $\sqrt[3]{2}$, $\sqrt[3]{3}$, and $\sqrt[3]{4}$, paired with
   linear scaling gauges for iterative doubling and halving of any given cube.
2. **Plato's Mechanical Carpenter's Square (pp. 163–164, Fig. 50)**: Fitting a grooved
   :term:`Winckelmeß <winckelmess>` ``r.p.q.`` with a sliding perpendicular cross-bar ``t.s.``
   realizes two coupled right triangles ``g.c.d.`` and ``c.d.e.``, proved rigorously via **Euclid
   VI.8** ($gb : cb = cb : db = db : eb$).
3. **Hero of Alexandria's Rectangular Neusis (pp. 164–165, Fig. 51)**: Pivoting a straightedge
   through corner ``b`` of rectangle ``a.b.g.d.`` until distances ``e.h.`` and ``e.z.`` from center
   ``e`` are equal yields two mean proportionals ``a.h.`` and ``g.z.``, proved step-by-step via
   **Euclid VI.2, VI.15, and VI.4**.

Cube Sciagraphy & Central Projection Perspective Apparatuses (Figs. 52–63)
==========================================================================

Book IV culminates in the **geometric synthesis of light, shadow, and central perspective**
(*Perspectiva = Durchsehung*):

1. **Two-View Cube Shadow Sciagraphy (pp. 166–169, Figs. 52–53)**:
   Placing a cube on square ground ``e.f.g.h.`` in both horizontal ground plan (*Cubus im Grund*,
   illuminated by plan light ``p``) and upright elevation (*Cubus aufrecht*, illuminated by
   elevation light ``o``), Dürer intersects elevation shadow-range verticals ``i, k`` with plan
   shadow rays through corners ``b2, c3, d4`` to determine the exact six-sided shadow polygon
   ``b2–l–m–n–d4``—reminding the reader that *"both plans and both lights are one single thing, split
   apart solely for ease of execution."*

2. **Transparent Picture Plane & Two Perspective Constructions (pp. 169–178, Figs. 54–62)**:
   Sight proceeds exclusively along rectilinear rays (*Streimlinien*) from the :term:`eye point
   <augpunct>`; inserting a **transparent planar section** (:term:`ebne durchsichtige Abschneydung
   <durchsichtige-abschneydung>`, :person:`Alberti's <alberti>` *intersegazione* :cite:`alberti:1435`)
   across the cone of rays captures the exact perspective image. Dürer demonstrates **two rigorous
   constructions** of the illuminated cube:

   - **First Method (pp. 170–175, Figs. 56–58)**: Coordinate transfer (:term:`Abstelen <abstelen>`)
     with *two compasses* from split elevation and ground-plan eye rays onto orthogonal crosshairs
     centered at the principal vanishing point.
   - **Second Shorter Method (pp. 175–178, Figs. 59–62)**: Direct planar construction on the picture
     sheet using the principal vanishing point (:g:`V_x`), a lateral distance point at equal height,
     and the $45^\circ$ ground diagonal ``e.g.``—projecting front face :g:`A' <F_BL>`–:g:`B'
     <F_BR>`–:g:`C' <F_TR>`–:g:`D' <F_TL>`, receding back face :g:`E' <B_BL>`–:g:`F' <B_BR>`–:g:`G'
     <B_TR>`–:g:`H' <B_TL>`, and foreshortened depth midpoint :g:`M' <M_depth>`.

.. picture:: ../../_static/figures/book4_perspective_cube_apparatus.svg
   :caption: Exact central projection of Dürer's cube onto the glass picture frame (Book IV, Figs. 56–63): receding depth edges (:g:`A' <F_BL>`–:g:`E' <B_BL>`, :g:`B' <F_BR>`–:g:`F' <B_BR>`, :g:`C' <F_TR>`–:g:`G' <B_TR>`, :g:`D' <F_TL>`–:g:`H' <B_TL>`) converge at principal vanishing point :g:`V_x` $= (3, 5)$, preserving the harmonic cross-ratio $\operatorname{CR}(A', E'; M', V_x) = -1$.

3. **The Glass Picture Frame & Three-Thread Lute Instruments (pp. 178–181, Figs. 62–63)**:
   To make central projection tangible to every painter, Dürer concludes with two celebrated
   mechanical instruments: (a) a **hinged glass picture frame** paired with a lead-screw and notched
   peep-sight rod (*gedreter stab*) holding the artist's eye fixed while tracing a portrait directly
   onto glass; and (b) the **three-thread sighting apparatus** (*durch drey feden*), in which an
   assistant stretches a weighted string from a wall needle ("the eye") through an open frame to
   successive points on a **foreshortened lute**, while the master marks each ray's intersection
   with wax-affixed cross-threads and swings shut a hinged panel door to prick the exact perspective
   coordinates!
