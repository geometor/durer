Two-View Orthographic Conic Sections & Kinematic Curves (Figs. 34–42)
=====================================================================

The ancients (:person:`Apollonius <apollonius>`, :cite:`apollonius:conics`) showed that one may
make **three distinct sections through a cone** whose curves differ from the circular base:

1. The first oblique section, cutting completely across the cone without touching its base, the
   learned call *Elipsis*; in German I shall name it an :term:`Eierlini <eierlini>` (*egg-line*).
2. The second section, drawn parallel (*ein barlini*) to one side of the cone, the learned call
   *Parabola*; let it be named a :term:`Brennspiegellini <brennspiegellini>` (*burning-mirror
   line*), because a hollow mirror shaped from it kindles fire at its focus.
3. The third section, drawn parallel to the vertical axis of the cone, they call *Hiperbole*; I
   shall name it a :term:`Gabellini <gabellini>` (*fork-line*).

To plot the *Elipsis* truthfully, one must first draw the **upright elevation cone**
(:term:`aufgezogner Grund <aufgezogner-grund>`) with apex :g:`a` and base :g:`b`–:g:`c`, mark the
oblique cutting segment :g:`f`–:g:`g` divided by eleven points into **twelve equal intervals**
(`1..11`), and directly beneath it construct the **circular ground plan**
(:term:`nidergedruckter Grund <nidergedruckter-grund>`, centered at :g:`O_plan`):

- At each numbered height `1..11` along :g:`f`–:g:`g`, measure with the compass the horizontal
  semi-radius of the cone from its axis out to its generator, carry (*abstelen*) that radius down
  into the ground plan around :g:`O_plan`, and intersect that concentric circle with the vertical
  projector dropped from station `k` on :g:`f`–:g:`g`.
- Erect a separate vertical axis equal in true length to :g:`f`–:g:`g` with eleven perpendicular
  cross-lines, transfer the chord half-widths cut off in the ground plan symmetrically to both
  sides of each station, and trace the curve point by point.

.. picture:: /_static/figures/book1_cone_ellipse_projection.svg
   :caption: Dürer's two-view orthographic descriptive geometry of the conic section (Book I, Fig. 34): elevation cone (:g:`a`, :g:`b`, :g:`c`) sliced along :g:`f`–:g:`g`, circular ground plan (:g:`O_plan`) below, and true-shape section proving exact bilateral symmetry $w_k = w_{12-k} = \frac{\sqrt{2k(12-k)}}{3}$.

Immediately after the three conic sections and the law of equal angles of incidence and
reflection for the paraboloidal burning mirror (Fig. 36), Dürer introduces three remarkable
kinematic curves and instruments:

- The **Muschellini** (*mussel-shell line*, Figs. 38–39, pp. 039–041), traced both pointwise on
  sixteen sliding stations and mechanically by a wheeled lance (*lentzlein*) riding in a grooved
  beam, adapting :person:`Nicomedes's <nicomedes>` conchoid principle.
- The **Spinnenlini** (*spider-line*, Fig. 40, p. 042), generated by a compound planetary motion
  in which arm ``bc`` rotates degree-for-degree around ``b`` while crank ``ab`` rotates around
  ``a``—an exact epitrochoid / limaçon a century before Étienne Pascal.
- A **four-jointed spatial linkage** (Figs. 41–42, pp. 043–045) whose discs and telescoping rods
  diminish in exact $\sqrt{2}$ geometric proportion to trace three-dimensional serpentine curves.
