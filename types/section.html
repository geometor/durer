First Printed Planar Fold-Out Nets for Platonic & Archimedean Solids (Figs. 29–43)

In the opening of Book IV, Dürer classifies three families of solid bodies that may be fashioned with compass and straightedge: prismatic columns of uniform cross-section, tapering cones and pyramids, and finally bodies equal on every side in their faces, solid corners, and edges, “which :person:`Euclid <euclid>` calls :term:`corpora regularia <corpora-regularia>`, describing five of them because there can be no more that may be inscribed in a sphere touching it at every vertex” (euclid:elements, plato:timaeus).

Notes — Combinatorics & Topology of Dürer’s Polyhedral Nets

Book IV (pp. 142–157) marks a watershed between Luca Pacioli's Divina Proportione (pacioli:1509)—where Leonardo da Vinci rendered polyhedra as perspective wooden frames—and Johannes Kepler's Harmonices Mundi Book II (kepler:1619), which completed the enumeration of all thirteen Archimedean solids.

Notes — Dedication to Willibald Pirckheimer (1525)

Dürer’s opening dedication to Nuremberg patrician and humanist Willibald Pirckheimer (1470–1530) articulates the core intellectual manifesto of the Northern Renaissance: liberating the German craftsman from unreflective workshop custom (wie eyn wylder vnbeschnytener bawm) through the mathematical rigor of compass and straightedge (die kunst der messung). Written amidst the religious upheavals of 1525, Dürer also mounts a spirited humanist defense of painting against Reformation iconoclasm, insisting that sacred art edifies rather than misleads when governed by rational proportion.

Notes — Plane Enclosure, Corners vs. Angles & Quadrilaterals (Figs. 1–8)

In Book II (Figs. 1–8), Dürer emphasizes the practical craftsman’s operational distinction between looking upon the sharp external vertex (Eck, salient angle) versus looking into the recessed hollow (Winckel, interior cavity). He grounds angle construction in Thales’s semicircle theorem (Fig. 4) and classifies eight species of quadrilaterals (Fierungen)—distinguishing the true square (rechte Fierung) from oblongs, rhombi, rhomboids, and four empirical trapezia.

Notes — Vitruvian Orders, Entasis & Enveloped-Sphere 3D Contours

Book III opens Dürer’s architectural treatise with an homage to Vitruvius (vitruvius:architectura), balanced by the German master’s flair for bold ornamental invention. To define the sinuous three-dimensional contour of a twisted column (Figs. 9–10), Dürer conceives every station along its coiled helical axis as the center of an inscribed sphere (ein yeklicher punct in der gewunden axt der seulen ein centrum einer kugel)—an early practical instance of envelope theory in spatial constructive geometry.

Notes — Algebraic Audit of Dürer’s Pentagon, Heptagon & 1525 Errata

Why did Dürer present two pentagon constructions back-to-back on pages 058–059 (Figs. 15 and 16)? While Figure 15 gives Ptolemy's exact golden-ratio chord over $\mathbb{Q}(\phi)$, Figure 16 requires never changing the compass opening (auß vnuerruckten zirckel)—a classic medieval stonemasons’ and Islamic workshop protocol going back to Abū al-Wafāʾ (abulwafa:craftsmen) and Matthäus Roriczer (roritzer:1487), which Hermann Staigmüller (staigmuller:1891) and Émile Lemoine (lemoine:1902) celebrated as a milestone of constructive simplicity.

Notes — Point, Elementary Lines & Parallel Curves (Figs. 1–5)

In translating Euclid's Elements Book I definitions (euclid:elements) into German print for the first time, Dürer invents a precise vernacular technical vocabulary: Punct, gerade Lini, Zirckellini, and equidistant Barlinien. Crucially, Dürer augments Euclid’s planar palette with the inflected Schlangenlini (serpentine S-curve) and observes the physical convergence of plumb lines toward the center of the earth—grounding abstract geometry directly in workshop observation.

Notes — Gothic Tracery, Planar Tilings & Pentagonal Parquetries (Figs. 21–27)

Dürer’s four assemblies of regular pentagons in Book II, Fig. 24 (p. 066)—including antiparallel strips and fivefold rosettes (fünf rosen van den fünf ecken) separated by golden rhombic voids—mark the historical birth of non-periodic fivefold planar parquetries.
